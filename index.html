1. Project Plan¶

Project Goal¶

Where you live predicts how long you live. Life expectancy can differ by more than a decade between neighboring U.S. counties and chronic conditions like diabetes & hypertension are distributed unevenly across the country.

This project investigates which county-level socioeconomic conditions predict chronic disease prevalence across all 3,100 U.S. counties. Obviously, the interesting question isn't whether poorer counties are less healthy, that's well established, but whether any other factor predicts health outcomes independently of income.

If education, insurance coverage or healthcare access still matter after controlling for income, that points to targeted interventions. If they don't, that suggests that much of what looks like a healthcare access problem is really just a poverty problem.

Selected Datasets¶

  • Dataset 1 - 2025 CDC PLACES (Local Data for Better Health)

  • Dataset 2 - 2025 County Health Rankings & Roadmaps


Question

Does any socioeconomic predictor retain significant explanatory power for chronic disease prevalence after controlling for median household income and rural/urban status?

How Data Addresses Our Question¶

Both datasets are published at county level and keyed on the five-digit FIPS county code, which makes them directly joinable. PLACES supplies our dependent variables (disease prevalence) while County Health Rankings supplies the independent variables (socioeconomic conditions). After merging on FIPS, each row represents one county with both its health outcomes and its community conditions attached.

Raw Dataset 1¶

Source: CDC PLACES County Data

Documentation: About PLACES

Data Description:

PLACES provides model-based small-area estimates of 40 health measures for every U.S. county, covering chronic disease outcomes, preventive service use, health risk behaviors, disability, health status and health-related social needs. Estimates are generated from Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) survey responses combined with Census population data and American Community Survey estimates.

Data Collection:

All 40 county measures come from one source: the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), a state-based random digit dial telephone survey of U.S. adults

# 1. Pad the county FIPS code to ensure it's a 5-digit string
places_clean = places.copy()

key_cols = [
    'countyfips',
    'stateabbr',
    'countyname',
    'totalpopulation',
    'diabetes_crudeprev',    # Verified Diabetes name
    'chd_crudeprev',         # Coronary Heart Disease
    'stroke_crudeprev',      # Stroke
    'cancer_crudeprev',      # Cancer
    'casthma_crudeprev',     # Current Asthma
    'copd_crudeprev',        # COPD
    'bphigh_crudeprev',      # High Blood Pressure
    'obesity_crudeprev'      # Obesity
]

# Subset dataset
places_subset = places_clean[key_cols].copy()

# Drop rows where essential health outcomes are completely missing before casting
prevalence_cols = [col for col in places_subset.columns if col.endswith('_crudeprev')]
places_subset = places_subset.dropna(subset=prevalence_cols, how='all')

# 3. Explicitly set correct datatypes
places_subset['countyfips'] = places_subset['countyfips'].astype(str)
places_subset['stateabbr'] = places_subset['stateabbr'].astype(str)
places_subset['countyname'] = places_subset['countyname'].astype(str)
places_subset['totalpopulation'] = places_subset['totalpopulation'].astype(int)

for col in prevalence_cols:
    places_subset[col] = pd.to_numeric(places_subset[col], errors='coerce')

# Print datatypes and info

Summary of Dataset 1 (PLACES) Cleaning & Structure¶

What was done during cleaning:¶

  1. Standardized County Identifiers: Padded the countyfips column with leading zeros to guarantee a uniform 5-digit string representation (e.g., '02020'), which is essential for error-free merging with socioeconomic datasets.
  2. Feature Selection: Filtered down from 167 variables to focus on 8 key chronic disease crude prevalence metrics (diabetes, chd, stroke, cancer, casthma, copd, bphigh, obesity) alongside basic identifiers (countyfips, stateabbr, countyname) and totalpopulation.
  3. Handling Missing Values: Dropped records that were completely missing crucial disease prevalence estimations (reducing the dataset from 3,143 to 2,956 valid counties).
  4. Explicit Datatype Alignment: Converted geographic codes and names into robust strings, cast populations to integers, and converted all prevalence metrics to float64 values using coerced numeric parsing.


What the cleaned dataset shows:¶

This dataset now contains a standardized 12-column record for 2,956 U.S. counties. For each county, it provides the estimated percentage (crude prevalence) of adults living with major chronic conditions.

3. Light EDA¶

  1. Diabetes prevalence by income group

Counties that are in the lowest income quartile average about a 13.68% diabetes prevalence but take that compared to a 9.44% in the highest income quartile. This difference of 4.24 points suggests that lower-income counties tend to have a higher diabetes prevalence. This is important because income is the variable Martin and I will control for in our analysis.

  1. Child poverty and diabetes prevalence

The relationship between child poverty and diabetes prevalence is around .827 showing theres a positive relationship between the two. Counties with higher percentages of children living in poverty tend to have higher adult diabetes percentages. This is important because child poverty may be reflecting broader economic disadvantages within a community.

  1. Education and diabetes prevalence

The correlation between the percentage of adults with some college education and not having diabetes is around -.649. Counties with higher levels of college education tend to have lower diabetes prevalence. This is important because education can be associated with access to resources poor counties do not have and health information.
